\title{A Connectome Test of the Fly Hashing Algorithm}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sebastian Senge}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The \textit{Drosophila} olfactory circuit has been characterised as a
locality-sensitive hash: a sparse binary expansion from ${\sim}50$ glomeruli
onto ${\sim}2000$ Kenyon cells, followed by winner-take-all sparsification,
which outperforms classical locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) at similarity
search. Because no reconstruction of the circuit existed when that result was
obtained, its projection matrix was generated at random, anchored to biology
only through the number of inputs each Kenyon cell receives. The MaleCNS
connectome now permits the substitution of measured connectivity for that
idealisation. Evaluating nearest-neighbour retrieval over measured odorant
responses, we find the architectural advantage intact ($4.8\times$ over
classical LSH), while the measured wiring performs marginally \emph{worse}
than a curveball null that fixes both degree sequences and randomises only
the pairing: a $90\%$ bootstrap interval over odorants places the difference
between $-2.2\%$ and $-0.3\%$ at the largest hash size. The protocol resolves
effects of $1.7\%$, and rejects a deliberately degraded matrix at $z<-100$, so
the interval reflects the circuit rather than the instrument. We do not claim
the wiring is unstructured, which independent work has refuted; we claim that
the structure it carries does not serve this computation, and costs a little
of it.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
connectomics, locality-sensitive hashing, similarity search, neural
architectures, null models
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Similarity search --- retrieving the database items most like a query --- is a
primitive of information retrieval, and locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) is its
standard sublinear solution \cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}. An LSH maps items to
short codes under which similar items collide with high probability, so that
expensive comparisons in the input space are replaced by cheap comparisons over
codes.

Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha \cite{dasgupta2017} observed that the fruit
fly's olfactory circuit computes something structurally analogous, and that it
departs from classical practice in three respects: its projection is sparse and
binary rather than dense and Gaussian, it expands dimensionality rather than
reducing it, and it sparsifies the expanded representation by winner-take-all.
Evaluated on standard retrieval benchmarks, this ``fly algorithm'' improved on
classical LSH by as much as $3\times$.

The projection matrix in that work was necessarily synthetic. Connectivity
between olfactory projection neurons and Kenyon cells had not been
reconstructed, so the authors sampled a sparse binary random matrix whose sole
biological anchor was a mean of roughly six inputs per Kenyon cell, consistent
with anatomical estimates. The choice was well motivated: the prevailing
account held such connectivity to be effectively random \cite{caron2013}.
Whether the idealisation was \emph{accurate}, as opposed to merely convenient,
could not be determined.

Two developments make the question answerable. Zheng \textit{et al.}
\cite{zheng2022} exploited the hemibrain volume to demonstrate that Kenyon
cells sample olfactory input in a measurably non-random fashion, displacing the
earlier account. Independently, the MaleCNS connectome \cite{malecns2026}
supplies a complete synaptic reconstruction of an adult central nervous system,
and with it the entire mushroom body input layer of a single animal.

Taken together these results pose a question that neither resolves. The wiring
is structured, and the circuit is argued to implement a hash; it does not
follow that the structure is \emph{for} the hash. Substituting measured
connectivity for the idealisation of \cite{dasgupta2017} therefore tests a
specific functional hypothesis rather than a general claim about randomness.
We report that the substitution yields no benefit, and a small reliable cost.
Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item the first evaluation of the fly hashing algorithm under measured rather
than sampled connectivity;
\item a null-model protocol that separates the contribution of wiring
\emph{pairing} from the degree sequences with which it is confounded, paired
with calibration matrices that establish the effect size the protocol can
resolve;
\item an equivalence-test formulation, with uncertainty propagated at the level
of odorants rather than queries, under which the measured matrix is bounded
within $2.2\%$ of its null --- on the deficit side.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Locality-Sensitive Hashing}

A family $\mathcal{H}$ of hash functions over a metric space $(\mathcal{X}, D)$
is $(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive if, for $h$ drawn uniformly from
$\mathcal{H}$,
\begin{align}
D(u,v) \le r &\;\Rightarrow\; \Pr[h(u) = h(v)] \ge p_1, \\
D(u,v) \ge cr &\;\Rightarrow\; \Pr[h(u) = h(v)] \le p_2,
\end{align}
with $c > 1$ and $p_1 > p_2$ \cite{indyk1998}. Classical constructions reduce
dimensionality with dense random projections and binarise, e.g. the sign of a
Gaussian projection \cite{charikar2002}.

\subsection{The Fly Circuit as a Hash}

Olfactory receptor neurons converge onto $d \approx 51$ glomeruli, whose
projection neurons diverge onto $m \approx 2000$ Kenyon cells. Two mechanisms
shape the code. Divisive normalisation in the antennal lobe removes
concentration, retaining odour identity \cite{olsen2010}. A single giant
inhibitory neuron, APL, implements approximate winner-take-all, leaving a few
percent of Kenyon cells active \cite{turner2008,lin2014}. The resulting sparse
binary pattern is the circuit's tag for an odour. The same construction also
supports novelty detection \cite{dasgupta2018}.

\subsection{Null Models for Connectomes}

Asking whether a measured network is ``special'' requires a null preserving
everything uninteresting. Degree-preserving randomisation \cite{maslov2002} is
standard: it retains each node's connection count while destroying which node
connects to which. In a bipartite projection, two degree sequences may be
preserved independently, and which one is held fixed changes the question
being asked.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The Measured Projection}

Let $M \in \{0,1\}^{m \times d}$ with $M_{ij} = 1$ iff glomerulus $j$ synapses
onto Kenyon cell $i$. Define the \emph{claw count} of cell $i$ and the
\emph{fan-out} of glomerulus $j$ as
\begin{equation}
c_i = \textstyle\sum_{j} M_{ij}, \qquad f_j = \textstyle\sum_{i} M_{ij}.
\end{equation}

We build $M$ from MaleCNS v1.0 \cite{malecns2026} using every reconstructed
connection, imposing no synapse-count threshold: the resulting graph has
$2.56 \times 10^7$ connections and $1.24 \times 10^8$ synapses. Thresholding is
common when connectomes drive dynamical models \cite{shiu2024}, but Kenyon cell
claws are individually weak, and a threshold removes them: at a $\ge 5$ synapse
cut the mean claw count falls to $4.79$, against $5.28$ with no cut and an
anatomical expectation near six.

Projection neurons are identified by the \texttt{ALPN} class and assigned to
glomeruli from their cell-type labels; multiglomerular and unnamed types are
excluded, as are thermo- and hygrosensory channels. Restricted to one
hemisphere this yields $d = 51$ glomeruli and $m = 1886$ Kenyon cells.

\subsection{The Hash}

Following \cite{dasgupta2017}, for an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}_{\ge 0}$ of
glomerular responses:
\begin{align}
\tilde{x} &= \frac{d}{\sum_{j} x_j}\, x
  &&\text{(divisive normalisation)}, \\
y &= M \tilde{x}
  &&\text{(sparse binary expansion)}, \\
h_k(x)_i &= \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, y_i \ge y_{(k)} \,\right]
  &&\text{(winner-take-all)},
\end{align}
where $y_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th largest entry of $y$. The tag $h_k(x) \in
\{0,1\}^m$ has exactly $k$ ones. Tags are compared by Hamming distance.

The classical baseline draws $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ with i.i.d.\
Gaussian entries and returns $\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{x}^\top W)$, so both methods
expend $k$ bits.

\subsection{Null and Calibration Models}

Only $M$ varies across conditions; input, normalisation, sparsity and scoring
are held fixed, so any difference is attributable to connectivity alone.

\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathcal{N}_{\text{claw}}$ preserves $\{c_i\}$ and draws each cell's
inputs uniformly without replacement, thereby flattening fan-out.
\item $\mathcal{N}_{\text{conf}}$ preserves \emph{both} $\{c_i\}$ and $\{f_j\}$
and randomises only the pairing. This is the null of record: a difference it
exposes cannot be ascribed to either degree sequence.
\item $\mathcal{N}_{6}$ reproduces the construction of \cite{dasgupta2017},
six uniformly drawn inputs per cell.
\end{itemize}

Sampling $\mathcal{N}_{\text{conf}}$ correctly requires care. Permuting edge
endpoints and discarding the resulting collisions is attractively simple but
loses several percent of the connections, and therefore samples from the wrong
space; the deficit systematically handicaps the null. We instead use curveball
swaps \cite{strona2014}: two Kenyon cells are drawn, the glomeruli unique to
each are pooled, and the pool is redealt between them at the original
cardinalities. Both margins are invariant by construction, which we verify
exactly, and $30$ attempted swaps per cell is well beyond the mixing time at
this density.

Two further matrices are not nulls but calibration probes, included to
establish what the protocol can detect. $\mathcal{P}_{\text{bal}}$ preserves
$\{c_i\}$ while equalising fan-out as far as the claw counts permit, and is
expected to be mildly superior; $\mathcal{P}_{\text{deg}}$ restricts every cell
to the same four glomeruli, and is expected to be far worse. Without such
probes a null result is uninterpretable, since an insensitive test and a true
absence of effect produce identical output.

\subsection{Evaluation}

For query $q$ let $\mathrm{NN}_\kappa(q)$ be its $\kappa$ nearest items by
Euclidean distance in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $R_\kappa(q)$ the $\kappa$ retrieved
by Hamming distance on tags, ranked. With
$\mathrm{rel}(i) = \mathbf{1}[R_\kappa(q)_i \in \mathrm{NN}_\kappa(q)]$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP}_\kappa(q) = \frac{1}{\kappa} \sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}
  \mathrm{rel}(i)\cdot \frac{\sum_{l \le i} \mathrm{rel}(l)}{i},
\end{equation}
and $\mathrm{mAP}$ averages over queries. We use $\kappa = 10$.

Two sources of variation must be carried. Retrieval quality varies across
draws of a null matrix, and it varies across the items queried; and because
queries are constructed from a smaller set of measured odorants, they are not
independent. We therefore resample \emph{odorants}, not queries: for each of
$2000$ bootstrap draws we sample odorants with replacement, restrict to the
queries they dominate, and recompute the difference between the measured
matrix and the mean over $B=40$ null draws.

Let $\Delta$ denote that difference and $\bar{s}_0$ the null mean. We report
the $90\%$ interval of $\Delta/\bar{s}_0$, and declare equivalence at margin
$\delta$ when the interval lies within $(-\delta, +\delta)$, which is the
two-one-sided-tests criterion. This matters: a non-significant difference is
not evidence of absence, whereas an interval bounded inside $\pm\delta$ is
evidence that no effect larger than $\delta$ is present. We also report
$z = (s(M_{\text{real}}) - \bar{s}_0)/\sigma_0$ for comparability with the
connectomics literature, and the two-sigma resolution
$\delta_{2\sigma} = 2\sigma_0/\bar{s}_0$.

\subsection{Data}

Odorant responses come from DoOR 2.0 \cite{munch2016}, mapped from receptor to
glomerulus. Its consensus matrix is ${\sim}17\%$ populated, so we retain
odorants measured across at least 12 glomeruli and glomeruli measured across at
least 40 odorants, giving $172$ odorants over $35$ glomeruli; the projection is
restricted accordingly to $35 \times 1838$. Because $172$ items leave the null
too broad to resolve small effects, queries are Dirichlet-weighted blends of
two to five measured odorants --- mixtures being what a fly actually encounters
--- yielding $n = 4000$ items that retain the natural correlation structure.

\subsection{The Architecture Reproduces Its Advantage}

Table~\ref{tab:map} and Fig.~\ref{fig:main} (left) show the expansion and
winner-take-all construction outperforming the Gaussian baseline at an equal
bit budget by a factor of $4.8$ averaged over hash sizes, qualitatively
reproducing \cite{dasgupta2017}. The reproduction is in kind rather than in
magnitude, and deliberately so: the measured matrix has fixed dimensions, so
the benchmarks of \cite{dasgupta2017} cannot be passed through it without
first projecting them into the circuit's input space, which would no longer
test the circuit. Our evaluation is therefore conducted at the fly's own scale
and in its own modality, where the comparison is meaningful.

\subsection{The Measured Wiring Confers No Advantage}

Against $\mathcal{N}_{\text{conf}}$ the measured matrix yields
$z = -1.11, -1.04, -1.47, -1.40$ at $k = 8, 16, 32, 64$, with $33, 35, 38, 38$
of $40$ null draws scoring higher. Under the odorant-level bootstrap the
$90\%$ interval on the relative difference is $[-6.9\%, -1.2\%]$,
$[-3.5\%, -0.4\%]$, $[-2.6\%, -0.2\%]$ and $[-2.2\%, -0.3\%]$ respectively.

Two things follow, and they should not be conflated. First, the interval
excludes zero at every hash size, so the deficit is reliable rather than
noise: measured connectivity is slightly \emph{worse} for retrieval than a
degree-matched random rewiring. Second, the deficit is small and bounded ---
within $2.2\%$ at $k=64$ --- so the measured matrix is equivalent to its null
at any margin a practitioner would plausibly care about. The appropriate
summary is not that wiring makes no difference, but that it makes a small
negative one.

\subsection{Calibration and Resolution}

Table~\ref{tab:sens} bounds the instrument. A degraded matrix is rejected at
$z$ between $-26$ and $-113$, so gross departures are detected without
difficulty. An improvement of the magnitude produced by
$\mathcal{P}_{\text{bal}}$ reaches $z = +1.40$ and is not resolved, consistent
with a two-sigma resolution of $1.7\%$ at $k=64$. The bootstrap interval is
tighter than this threshold because it pools information across queries rather
than across matrices, which is why we treat it, and not $z$, as the primary
statistic.

\subsection{Robustness}

The conclusion is stable under the choices that might plausibly have driven
it. Imposing a $\ge 5$ synapse reconstruction threshold reduces the graph
fourfold, to $6.24 \times 10^6$ connections, and moves the mean claw count from
$5.28$ to $4.79$; $z$ remains within $[-1.82, -1.12]$. The result also holds
for the contralateral mushroom body, for synapse-weighted rather than binary
$M$, and for Gaussian, low-rank and sparse synthetic inputs, over which $z$
ranges in $[-0.81, +1.25]$ without consistent sign.

One asymmetry deserves separate record, because it suggests where the deficit
originates. Measured fan-out is strongly non-uniform: DA1, the glomerulus
carrying the pheromone cVA, drives $473$ Kenyon cells, while the least
connected drives $19$. Both $\mathcal{N}_{\text{claw}}$ and
$\mathcal{P}_{\text{bal}}$, which flatten this distribution while leaving claw
counts intact, score above the measured matrix at every $k$. Disproportionate
allocation of mushroom body capacity to behaviourally privileged channels is
precisely the kind of structure reported by \cite{zheng2022}, and on this
evidence it is paid for in general-purpose retrieval.

\section{Discussion}

Our result should not be read as a claim that projection-neuron to Kenyon-cell
connectivity is random. Zheng \textit{et al.} \cite{zheng2022} established
that it is not, and the fan-out asymmetry documented above is itself
structure, visible in the same data we analyse. The claim is narrower: the
structure that is present confers no advantage on similarity search, the
computation the circuit has been argued to perform, and in fact exacts a
small cost.

This reconciles the two literatures rather than opposing either. The
idealisation adopted in \cite{dasgupta2017} was not merely expedient but
substantively adequate for its purpose, since with respect to retrieval
quality a measured matrix and a degree-matched random one are interchangeable
to within a few percent. Where a computation requires a random projection,
evolution appears to have supplied something close enough to one; the
departures from randomness that do exist are then free to serve other ends,
of which innate valence, developmental constraint and the over-allocation
visible in fan-out are the obvious candidates. A circuit can implement an
algorithm well without its every structural feature being an adaptation for
that algorithm, and distinguishing the two requires exactly the kind of null
the connectome now makes available.

The methodological point generalises beyond olfaction. Connectome-constrained
models are increasingly used to ground computational claims
\cite{shiu2024,lappalainen2024}, and such claims are testable in a way they
recently were not: fix every component of a circuit-derived algorithm except
its measured connectivity, randomise that against nulls which preserve the
uninteresting invariants, and calibrate the comparison against matrices whose
quality is known in advance. Absent the last step, a null result is
indistinguishable from an insensitive measurement.

\section{Limitations}

The central claim is a bounded difference rather than an absence: effects
smaller than roughly two percent are not excluded, and tightening the bound
requires more queries, which is quadratic in the retrieval evaluation. We
examine a single task under a single metric; novelty detection
\cite{dasgupta2018}, the circuit's other documented function, may weight
connectivity differently and is untested here. DoOR covers $35$ of $51$
glomeruli, so the projection is evaluated on a subset of its input channels,
and the $4000$ queries are mixtures of $172$ measured odorants rather than
independent measurements --- which the odorant-level bootstrap accounts for in
its uncertainty, but which still limits the effective sample. MaleCNS
reconstructs one male animal; whether the conclusion holds across individuals,
or against the female FlyWire reconstruction \cite{dorkenwald2024}, remains
open, and a cross-sex comparison is now feasible.

\section{Conclusion}

Replacing the random projection assumed in \cite{dasgupta2017} with measured
mushroom body connectivity leaves similarity-search performance within $2.2\%$
of a degree-matched null, on the deficit side, while the architectural
advantage over classical LSH is undiminished. The mushroom body is an
effective hash function by virtue of its shape rather than its wiring, and the
structure its wiring does carry appears to serve some other purpose at a
modest cost to this one. Code, data pipeline and results are available at
\url{https://github.com/ssenge/FlyHash-Connectome}.
