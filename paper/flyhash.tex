\title{A Connectome Test of the Fly Hashing Algorithm}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sebastian Senge}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha (2017) showed that the \textit{Drosophila}
olfactory circuit, modelled as a random sparse projection followed by
winner-take-all, is a locality-sensitive hash that beats classical LSH. The
projection was random because the wiring was unknown. We test it against four
electron-microscopy connectomes (MaleCNS, hemibrain, FlyWire, BANC; four
animals, seven hemispheres) and report three findings. First, the 2017 pattern
holds in a reimplementation of its protocol on SIFT, GloVe, MNIST and odour
mixtures: the fly hash beats $k$ Gaussian projections at short hash lengths
($\ReplMnistRatio\times$ in AP@200 on MNIST at $k=4$). Second, against the tested real-valued Gaussian baseline that advantage is per
active cell, not per operation: Gaussian projections given the same projection
arithmetic retrieve better on every dataset and at every input dimension
tested. Third, across four connectomes the measured pairing of glomeruli gives
no consistent retrieval advantage over degree-preserving rewiring: retrieval
is slightly lower (median \AnimRelMedian{}), and the small odour deficits
depend on how missing odour responses are treated. Separately, equalising
glomerular fan-out at fixed connection count improves retrieval in the model in
every hemisphere, while equalising inputs per cell lowers it on average.
\end{abstract}

connectomics, locality-sensitive hashing, similarity search, replication

\section{Introduction}

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) maps items to short codes under which similar
items collide with high probability \cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}. Dasgupta
\textit{et al.} \cite{dasgupta2017} noticed that the fly olfactory circuit
computes a related transform (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}a): about $50$ glomeruli
project through a sparse binary matrix onto about $2{,}000$ Kenyon cells, and
inhibition keeps the most active $5\%$. On SIFT, GloVe and MNIST this fly hash
beat classical LSH by up to about $3\times$. The projection was random,
following the account that the wiring is effectively random \cite{caron2013}.

That account has since been revised: projection-neuron input to Kenyon cells is
structured \cite{zheng2022}, and four connectomes now give the complete
projection \cite{malecns2026,scheffer2020,dorkenwald2024,bates2026}. We ask
whether the random matrix was a harmless idealisation for the task that
motivated it; \cite{zheng2022} studied odour discrimination, not retrieval.
Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item a reimplementation of the 2017 protocol with documented provenance,
extended with odour mixtures, that recovers the published pattern;
\item a budget analysis showing that, against real-valued Gaussian projections,
the fly hash's advantage is per active cell and not per operation;
\item a comparison of the fly hash across four connectomes and seven
hemispheres, on the 2017 benchmarks and on measured odour responses, against
degree-preserving rewiring and against controls that change one degree
sequence at a time at equal connection count.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\emph{Connectomes.} A connectome is a wiring diagram at the resolution of
individual neurons and synapses. It is reconstructed from electron-microscopy
images of a whole brain or nervous system: every neuron is traced and every
synapse between two neurons is detected and counted. For the adult fruit fly
this became feasible only recently; the four connectomes used here each contain
the complete mushroom body, the part of the brain studied in this paper.

\emph{The fly olfactory pathway.} An odour activates olfactory receptor neurons
in the antennae. Neurons that express the same receptor type all converge onto
one small, anatomically identifiable structure in the antennal lobe, a
\emph{glomerulus}; the fly has about $50$ olfactory glomeruli, so an odour is
first represented as a vector of about $50$ glomerular activities. Projection
neurons carry each glomerulus's signal to the mushroom body, where about
$2{,}000$ \emph{Kenyon cells} per hemisphere each receive input from a handful
of glomeruli. A single inhibitory neuron (APL) feeds back onto all Kenyon cells,
so that only the most strongly driven few percent fire. The resulting sparse
set of active Kenyon cells is the fly's internal code for the odour and the
basis for associative memory \cite{dasgupta2017,caron2013}. In this paper $M$ is
the glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell part of that pathway: which glomeruli each
Kenyon cell receives input from.

\emph{Similarity search and hashing.} Nearest-neighbour search asks, for a
query item, which stored items are most similar to it. Exhaustive comparison
is expensive for large collections, so approximate methods first map items to
short \emph{hashes} and compare those instead. A hash is locality-sensitive
if similar items receive similar hashes with high probability
\cite{indyk1998}. The classical construction multiplies the input by a random
Gaussian matrix and keeps the $k$ projected values, or their signs, as the hash
\cite{charikar2002}. The fly hash of \cite{dasgupta2017} replaces the dense
matrix with the sparse binary glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell matrix, expands rather
than compresses the dimension, and keeps as the hash the identities of the $k$
most active Kenyon cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}a). Retrieval quality is the
agreement between the neighbours found through the hashes and the true
neighbours.

\section{Related work}

The fly hash has been extended with dense pseudo-hashes and multi-probe
binning \cite{sharma2018}, and with projections learned by local plasticity
rules \cite{ryali2020,liang2021}; these change the algorithm, whereas we keep
it and change only the matrix. On the biological side, random convergence of
projection-neuron input was reported from anatomical tracing
\cite{caron2013}; electron microscopy later mapped the mushroom body
completely in the larva \cite{eichler2017} and in the adult hemibrain
\cite{li2020}, and found structured sampling in FAFB, where simulated odour
discrimination was worse than with random wiring \cite{zheng2022}. None of
these studies evaluated similarity search, the task behind the hashing
interpretation.


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Connectomes and matrices}

Let $M\in\{0,1\}^{d\times m}$ with $M_{ji}=1$ iff glomerulus $j$ connects to
Kenyon cell $i$. For every connectome the same rule applies: uniglomerular
olfactory projection neurons are assigned to glomeruli by cell type
(multiglomerular, thermo- and hygrosensory types excluded), Kenyon cells are
those annotated on the same side, every reconstructed connection counts (no
synapse threshold), and cells without olfactory input are dropped. Hemibrain
glomerulus names are mapped to current nomenclature via the cross-dataset
matches of \cite{schlegel2024}. We use MaleCNS v1.0 (male, both hemispheres),
hemibrain v1.2 (female, right), FlyWire v783 and BANC v888 (female, both). All
share $\AnimCommon$ glomeruli; they have \AnimCellsMin{} to \AnimCellsMax{}
Kenyon cells with \AnimInMin{} to \AnimInMax{} inputs per cell. The datasets
differ in imaging, synapse detection and proofreading, so part of any
difference between them is technical. The primary connectome is MaleCNS right
($d=\NGlomFull$, $m=\NCellsFull$).

Only $M$ changes between conditions. \emph{The null} is uniform over binary
matrices with the same row and column sums as $M$, sampled by curveball trades
\cite{strona2014,carstens2015} (30 per cell; co-occurrence
$Q=\sum_{j<l}(MM^\top)_{jl}^2$ is stable from \PlateauSweeps{} sweep on); it
keeps both degree sequences and randomises which glomeruli share a cell, and
tests against it are approximate Monte Carlo randomization tests.
\emph{Equal-connection controls} have exactly $\mathrm{nnz}(M)$ ones with
inputs per cell made even, fan-out made even, or both, and random pairing.
\emph{The 2017 construction} gives each cell six uniform inputs, changing both
degree sequences and the connection count.

\subsection{Hash, scores and budgets}

The fly hash computes $y=M^\top x$ and sets the $k$ largest entries of $y$ to
one; cells tied at the threshold drive are taken in index order (a random
tie priority is a sensitivity analysis). Retrieval is scored by $\mathrm{AP@}n=\frac1n\sum_i
\mathrm{prec@}i\cdot\mathrm{rel}(i)$ over the $n$ true neighbours, so neighbours
never retrieved count as misses, together with recall@$n$. LSH computes $k$
Gaussian projections kept as real values, as in the 2017 comparison, or their
signs for bit codes. Budgets are per active cell ($k$ projections for $k$
winners), per stored bit, and per operation, counted as projection arithmetic
as in \cite{dasgupta2017}: $d$ multiplications and $d$ additions per
projection, one addition per non-zero of $M$; winner selection, retrieval and
any PCA fit are excluded.

Table~\ref{tab:prov} gives the protocol and the source of each element; we add
$10{,}000$ mixtures of DoOR odour profiles as a fourth dataset. The 2017 paper
reports ``mean average precision'' without a formula. Precision averaged over
\emph{retrieved} true neighbours only, a convention in parts of the hashing
literature, reproduces its LSH baseline (MNIST, $k=4$: \ReplMnistLshRetr{}
against $0.160$) but not its fly score (\ReplMnistFlyRetr{} against $0.448$).
That score ignores misses, so we report AP@200 and recall@200 and compare with
the paper through orderings and ratios. With a connectome, $M$ accepts only its
own glomeruli: odours use the glomeruli DoOR covers, and SIFT, GloVe and MNIST
are reduced by PCA to one component per glomerulus of that connectome ($49$ or
$51$), assigned to glomeruli by a fresh random permutation in each trial, so these datasets test the wiring's
statistics, not its odour-specific pairing. Each connectome is scored against
$\ConnB$ nulls and one draw of each control per trial.

\subsection{Approach 2: odour analysis}

Responses are the DoOR 2.0 consensus matrix \cite{munch2016} (commit
\texttt{\DoorCommit}). Coverage is uneven: $35$ glomeruli are measured for $51$
or more odorants, eleven for $12$ to $24$, five for none. We keep the $35$ (our
threshold of $40$ odorants falls in that gap) and odorants measured on at least
$12$ of them: $\NOdorants$ odorants, \MissingPct{} of entries unmeasured and set
to zero. The benchmark is $\NItems$ linear mixtures of two to five profiles,
normalised to unit mean as in the circuit, with the $\kappa=10$ nearest items
on raw responses as ground truth. The primary size $k=\PrimaryK$ ($5\%$ of
cells) and secondary sizes $\{8,16,32,64\}$ were fixed in an earlier revision,
after first results had been seen; this is not a preregistration. MaleCNS is
tested against \NullB{} nulls with a two-stage bootstrap (resampled odorants,
regenerated mixtures, $20$ nulls drawn from the same pool per replicate,
$\BootR$ replicates) and a $\pm\MarginPrimary$ equivalence margin; every
hemisphere is tested against \AnimB{} nulls on its own and on the
$\AnimCommonOdour$ shared DoOR glomeruli.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The 2017 pattern holds}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_replication.pdf}}
\caption{The 2017 protocol with random matrices: AP@200 against hash length,
mean $\pm$ SD of \ReplTrials{} trials. Random tag selection performs like LSH;
winner-take-all at $m=20k$ and $10d$ beats both at short hash lengths.}
\label{fig:repl}

With random matrices the published ordering is recovered on SIFT, MNIST and
odours (Fig.~\ref{fig:repl}): random tag selection performs like LSH, as
\cite{dasgupta2017} argue it must, winner-take-all at $m=20k$ beats both, and
$m=10d$ does better still. The advantage is largest at short hash lengths
($\ReplMnistRatio\times$ on MNIST at $k=4$; recall $\ReplMnistRecallRatio\times$;
$\ReplOdourRatio\times$ on odours) and shrinks with $k$; on odours and GloVe
LSH overtakes the fly hash from $k\approx8$--$16$. Scoring against neighbours
on centred input, as the FlyLSH code does, leaves the MNIST ratio at
$\ReplMnistRatioCentred\times$. GloVe is close to chance at small $k$ for every
method.

\subsection{Against real-valued LSH, the advantage is per active cell, not per operation}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_budget.pdf}}
\caption{(a) One dataset at several input dimensions (MNIST reduced by PCA to
$d$; PCA also changes the retained information and the integer sampling rate),
$k=4$: the fly hash's lead over LSH per active cell grows with $d$, but LSH
given the same projection arithmetic as the $m=10d$ fly leads at every $d$.
(b, c) Best AP of the fly hash over the tested hash lengths against real-valued
LSH with the same projection arithmetic: (b) random matrices on the original
inputs, (c) the MaleCNS connectome on its own inputs. Inputs and ground truth
differ between (b) and (c); only pairs within a panel are comparable.}
\label{fig:budget}

The 2017 comparison sets $k$ active cells against $k$ projections, which
equates projection arithmetic only at $m=20k$. The headline network has
$m=10d$ cells sampling about $d/10$ inputs each: roughly $d^2$ additions
against $2kd$ for LSH, a factor near $d/2k$ ($98\times$ on MNIST at $k=4$). Varying
the input dimension of one dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}a) shows both sides:
per active cell, the fly hash trails LSH at $d=8$, ties at $16$ and leads from
$32$ on, though not monotonically; given equal arithmetic, real-valued LSH
leads at every $d$. With a random tie priority among Kenyon cells the fly/LSH
ratio at $k=4$ is \TieRatioEight{} at $d=8$ and \TieRatioSixteen{} at $d=16$
(index order: \IdxRatioEight{} and \IdxRatioSixteen{}): the fly hash trails at
$d=8$ and reaches parity by $d=16$ under both rules, but the value at $d=8$
depends on the rule. The same holds on all
four datasets (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}b, c), for random matrices (LSH
\ReplOpsMin{}--\ReplOpsMax{} AP) and for the connectome, whose
$\mathrm{nnz}(M)$ additions buy $\ConnOpsBits$ projections on image and word
input and $\ConnOdourOpsBits$ on odours; these beat it on \ConnOpsWins{} of
\ConnNData{} datasets. ``Best'' is the best over the tested hash lengths.


One-bit codes are weaker than real-valued projections, and against them the
comparison is closer (Table~\ref{tab:arch}): on odours the fly hash beats sign
codes of $k$ bits ($\ArchKbitsRatio\times$ at the primary size), trails at
matched storage ($\ArchStorageRatio\times$), and at matched operations trails
for small $k$ and leads from $k=32$ ($\ArchComputeRatio\times$).

\subsection{Equalising fan-out improves retrieval in the model}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_controls.pdf}}
\caption{Equal-connection degree controls, AP@200 relative to the null mean
(ratio of trial means), with $95\%$ bootstrap intervals over trials, each trial
paired with its own null mean. (a) MaleCNS right at the largest hash size
(\CtrlTrials{} trials, $\CtrlB$ nulls). (b) All hemispheres, averaged over the
four datasets and six hash sizes.}
\label{fig:ctrl}
\end{figure*}

The comparison with the null concerns the pairing alone: connectome and null
share both degree sequences, so neither sequence can explain the difference
between them. We asked separately whether the measured degree sequences limit
retrieval relative to more regular random matrices at the same connection count,
changing one sequence at a time (Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl}). These are interventions
within the model, compared with each trial's own null mean.

In MaleCNS right, making fan-out even, so that every glomerulus reaches the
same number of Kenyon cells, raises AP on image and word input by \CtrlOutMin{}
to \CtrlOutMax{} across datasets and hash sizes, with the interval above zero in
\CtrlOutPos{} of \CtrlCells{} cases, and on odours by \CtrlOutOdMin{} to
\CtrlOutOdMax{} (\CtrlOutOdPos{} of \CtrlOdCells{}). Making inputs per cell even
has mixed effects: \CtrlInMin{} to \CtrlInMax{} on image and word input (interval
above zero in \CtrlInPos{}, below in \CtrlInNeg{}), and \CtrlInOdMin{} to
\CtrlInOdMax{} on odours, largest at the smallest hash sizes. Averaged over
datasets and sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl}b), even fan-out raises AP in all
\AnimCtrlN{} hemispheres (\AnimOutLo{} to \AnimOutHi{}, every interval above zero),
whereas even inputs per cell lowers it in all \AnimCtrlN{} (\AnimInLo{} to
\AnimInHi{}, every interval below zero); its gains are confined to some small
hash sizes. The six-input
construction of 2017 behaves like the even-fan-out control. The measured
fan-out is strongly skewed (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}b; up to $\FanMax$ cells for
\FanMaxGlom{} against a mean near $195$). This shows that more even fan-out would
retrieve better at equal cost in this model, not that the skew is a biological
cost: it may serve other ends.

Row-centring, part of the 2017 protocol, alters winner order only when inputs
per cell differ. Removing it changes the connectome's AP on image and word input
by \NoCentSmallMin{} to \NoCentSmallMax{} at the smallest hash size and by at most
\NoCentLargeAbs{} at the largest, and changes the odour results strongly at small
$k$, where drive on non-negative input then tracks the number of inputs
(supplement).

\section{Discussion}

Under these retrieval protocols, the measured pairing gives no consistent
advantage over degree-preserving random wiring: four connectomes keep the fly
hash's per-cell advantage and retrieve within a few percent of their rewirings,
slightly lower, in the direction reported for odour discrimination by
\cite{zheng2022}; the small odour deficits depend on the input model. The
2017 construction, which differs from both in its degree sequences, retrieves
better than either, and the controls attribute that mainly to its even fan-out.
The measured skew in fan-out may serve other ends, such as innate valence
\cite{zheng2022} or novelty detection \cite{dasgupta2018}, which we do not
test.

The algorithmic claim needs a qualifier. Against the tested real-valued Gaussian
baseline the fly hash's advantage is per active cell: Gaussian projections given the same projection arithmetic
retrieve better on every dataset and input dimension we tested. Against one-bit
codes, and at the circuit's own scale, the comparison is closer. The finding on
fan-out suggests an improvement, a fly hash with even glomerular fan-out at
equal cost, which we leave to future work.

\section{Limitations}

Four animals, one male; differences between connectomes mix biology and
reconstruction. Our reimplementation recovers the published pattern but not
its absolute values, and two protocol details come from later code. Image and
word input is compressed to one PCA component per glomerulus and assigned to
glomeruli arbitrarily. The cross-connectome protocol runs use $\AnimBBench$ nulls and are
effect estimates. The odour deficit depends on treating unmeasured DoOR entries
as zero. The model is binary and rate-free and ignores inhibition beyond
winner-take-all.

\section{Conclusion}

In a documented reimplementation, sparse winner-take-all codes outperform equally
many real Gaussian projections at short hash lengths, while the tested Gaussian
baseline performs better at matched projection arithmetic. Across four
connectomes, the measured pairing provides no consistent retrieval advantage
over degree-preserving rewiring under the tested inputs; the small odour
deficits depend on the treatment of missing responses. Separately, equalising
glomerular fan-out at fixed connection count improves retrieval in the model,
in every hemisphere; equalising inputs per cell does not.

\url{https://github.com/ssenge/FlyHash-Connectome}.
