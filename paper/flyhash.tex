\title{Random Enough: A Connectome Test of the\\Fly Hashing Algorithm}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sebastian Senge}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The \textit{Drosophila} olfactory circuit has been characterised as a
locality-sensitive hash: a sparse expansion from ${\sim}50$ glomeruli onto
${\sim}2000$ Kenyon cells followed by winner-take-all sparsification, which
outperforms classical locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) at similarity search.
That result was obtained with a \emph{randomly generated} projection matrix,
because no connectome of the circuit existed. The MaleCNS v1.0 connectome makes
the assumption testable. We substitute the measured glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell
matrix for the random one and evaluate nearest-neighbour retrieval on measured
odorant responses. The architecture reproduces its advantage over classical LSH
($4.8\times$). The measured wiring, however, confers no advantage over a
configuration-model null that preserves both degree sequences and randomises
only the pairing: $z \in [-1.59, -1.12]$, with 35--38 of 40 null draws scoring
higher. Calibrating against matrices of known quality bounds any true advantage
below $1.8\%$ relative. This is not a claim that the wiring is unstructured;
independent work has shown that it is not. It is the narrower and, we argue,
more informative claim that the structure present is not structure that helps
this computation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
connectomics, locality-sensitive hashing, similarity search, neural
architectures, null models
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Similarity search --- given a query, return the database items most like it ---
is a primitive of information retrieval, and locality-sensitive hashing (LSH)
is its standard sublinear solution \cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}. An LSH maps
items to short codes such that similar items collide with high probability,
replacing expensive comparisons in the input space with cheap ones over codes.

Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha \cite{dasgupta2017} observed that the fruit
fly's olfactory circuit computes something structurally similar, and departs
from classical LSH in three ways: the projection is \emph{sparse} and
\emph{binary} rather than dense and Gaussian; it \emph{expands} rather than
reduces dimensionality; and it sparsifies the result by winner-take-all. On
standard benchmarks, their ``fly algorithm'' outperformed classical LSH by up
to $3\times$.

Their model of the projection was necessarily synthetic. The
glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell connectivity had not been mapped, so they drew a
sparse binary random matrix whose only biological anchor was that each Kenyon
cell samples roughly six inputs, matching anatomical estimates. The prevailing
view at the time held that this connectivity is effectively random
\cite{caron2013}, which made the choice defensible; whether it was
\emph{accurate} was untestable.

Two developments make it testable. First, Zheng \textit{et al.}
\cite{zheng2022} used the hemibrain volume to show that Kenyon cell sampling of
olfactory input is measurably \emph{not} random, overturning the earlier view.
Second, the MaleCNS v1.0 connectome \cite{malecns2026} provides a complete
reconstruction of an adult central nervous system, including the full
mushroom body input layer.

These two facts create a question that neither answers alone. The wiring is
structured; the circuit is claimed to implement a hash. \emph{Does the
structure help the hash?} We find that it does not, to within a $1.8\%$
resolution bound, while the architecture's advantage over classical LSH
reproduces. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item the first evaluation of the fly hashing algorithm using measured rather
than synthetic connectivity;
\item a null-model protocol that isolates wiring \emph{pairing} from the degree
sequences it is confounded with, together with calibration matrices that
establish what effect size the protocol can resolve;
\item the finding that measured structure yields no similarity-search
advantage, which retroactively validates the modelling assumption in
\cite{dasgupta2017} while constraining the functional interpretation of the
structure reported in \cite{zheng2022}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Locality-Sensitive Hashing}

A family $\mathcal{H}$ of hash functions over a metric space $(\mathcal{X}, D)$
is $(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive if, for $h$ drawn uniformly from
$\mathcal{H}$,
\begin{align}
D(u,v) \le r &\;\Rightarrow\; \Pr[h(u) = h(v)] \ge p_1, \\
D(u,v) \ge cr &\;\Rightarrow\; \Pr[h(u) = h(v)] \le p_2,
\end{align}
with $c > 1$ and $p_1 > p_2$ \cite{indyk1998}. Classical constructions reduce
dimensionality with dense random projections and binarise, e.g. the sign of a
Gaussian projection \cite{charikar2002}.

\subsection{The Fly Circuit as a Hash}

Olfactory receptor neurons converge onto $d \approx 51$ glomeruli, whose
projection neurons diverge onto $m \approx 2000$ Kenyon cells. Two mechanisms
shape the code. Divisive normalisation in the antennal lobe removes
concentration, retaining odour identity \cite{olsen2010}. A single giant
inhibitory neuron, APL, implements approximate winner-take-all, leaving a few
percent of Kenyon cells active \cite{turner2008,lin2014}. The resulting sparse
binary pattern is the circuit's tag for an odour. The same construction also
supports novelty detection \cite{dasgupta2018}.

\subsection{Null Models for Connectomes}

Asking whether a measured network is ``special'' requires a null preserving
everything uninteresting. Degree-preserving randomisation \cite{maslov2002} is
standard: it retains each node's connection count while destroying which node
connects to which. In a bipartite projection, two degree sequences may be
preserved independently, and which one is held fixed changes the question
being asked.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The Measured Projection}

Let $M \in \{0,1\}^{m \times d}$ with $M_{ij} = 1$ iff glomerulus $j$ synapses
onto Kenyon cell $i$. Define the \emph{claw count} of cell $i$ and the
\emph{fan-out} of glomerulus $j$ as
\begin{equation}
c_i = \textstyle\sum_{j} M_{ij}, \qquad f_j = \textstyle\sum_{i} M_{ij}.
\end{equation}

We build $M$ from MaleCNS v1.0 \cite{malecns2026} using every reconstructed
connection, imposing no synapse-count threshold: the resulting graph has
$2.56 \times 10^7$ connections and $1.24 \times 10^8$ synapses. Thresholding is
common when connectomes drive dynamical models \cite{shiu2024}, but Kenyon cell
claws are individually weak, and a threshold removes them: at a $\ge 5$ synapse
cut the mean claw count falls to $4.79$, against $5.28$ with no cut and an
anatomical expectation near six.

Projection neurons are identified by the \texttt{ALPN} class and assigned to
glomeruli from their cell-type labels; multiglomerular and unnamed types are
excluded, as are thermo- and hygrosensory channels. Restricted to one
hemisphere this yields $d = 51$ glomeruli and $m = 1886$ Kenyon cells.

\subsection{The Hash}

Following \cite{dasgupta2017}, for an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}_{\ge 0}$ of
glomerular responses:
\begin{align}
\tilde{x} &= \frac{d}{\sum_{j} x_j}\, x
  &&\text{(divisive normalisation)}, \\
y &= M \tilde{x}
  &&\text{(sparse binary expansion)}, \\
h_k(x)_i &= \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, y_i \ge y_{(k)} \,\right]
  &&\text{(winner-take-all)},
\end{align}
where $y_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th largest entry of $y$. The tag $h_k(x) \in
\{0,1\}^m$ has exactly $k$ ones. Tags are compared by Hamming distance.

The classical baseline draws $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ with i.i.d.\
Gaussian entries and returns $\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{x}^\top W)$, so both methods
expend $k$ bits.

\subsection{Null and Calibration Models}

Only $M$ varies; input, normalisation, sparsity and scoring are held fixed.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathcal{N}_{\text{claw}}$: preserves $\{c_i\}$; each cell's inputs are
drawn uniformly without replacement. Fan-out is flattened.
\item $\mathcal{N}_{\text{conf}}$: a configuration model preserving
\emph{both} $\{c_i\}$ and $\{f_j\}$, randomising only the pairing. This is the
null of record: any difference it exposes cannot be attributed to either
degree sequence.
\item $\mathcal{N}_{6}$: the construction of \cite{dasgupta2017}, six uniformly
drawn inputs per cell.
\end{itemize}
Two further matrices are not nulls but calibration probes, used to establish
what the protocol can detect:
$\mathcal{P}_{\text{bal}}$, which preserves $\{c_i\}$ while making fan-out as
uniform as possible, and $\mathcal{P}_{\text{deg}}$, in which every cell draws
from the same four glomeruli.

\subsection{Evaluation}

For query $q$ let $\mathrm{NN}_\kappa(q)$ be its $\kappa$ nearest items by
Euclidean distance in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $R_\kappa(q)$ the $\kappa$ retrieved
by Hamming distance on tags, ranked. With
$\mathrm{rel}(i) = \mathbf{1}[R_\kappa(q)_i \in \mathrm{NN}_\kappa(q)]$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP}_\kappa(q) = \frac{1}{\kappa} \sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}
  \mathrm{rel}(i)\cdot \frac{\sum_{l \le i} \mathrm{rel}(l)}{i},
\end{equation}
and $\mathrm{mAP}$ averages over queries. We use $\kappa = 10$.

Writing $s(M)$ for the mAP achieved by matrix $M$, and $\mu, \sigma$ for the
mean and standard deviation of $s$ over $B = 40$ draws from
$\mathcal{N}_{\text{conf}}$, we report
\begin{equation}
z = \frac{s(M_{\text{real}}) - \mu}{\sigma},
\qquad
\delta_{2\sigma} = \frac{2\sigma}{\mu}.
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_{2\sigma}$ is the smallest relative advantage the run could
resolve at two standard deviations; a true advantage must be smaller than it.
Reporting $\delta_{2\sigma}$ converts an unfalsifiable negative into a bound.

\subsection{Data}

Odorant responses come from DoOR 2.0 \cite{munch2016}, mapped from receptor to
glomerulus. Its consensus matrix is ${\sim}17\%$ populated, so we retain
odorants measured across at least 12 glomeruli and glomeruli measured across at
least 40 odorants, giving $172$ odorants over $35$ glomeruli; the projection is
restricted accordingly to $35 \times 1838$. Because $172$ items leave the null
too broad to resolve small effects, queries are Dirichlet-weighted blends of
two to five measured odorants --- mixtures being what a fly actually encounters
--- yielding $n = 4000$ items that retain the natural correlation structure.

\subsection{The Architecture Reproduces Its Advantage}

Table~\ref{tab:map} and Fig.~\ref{fig:main} (left) show the expansion plus
winner-take-all construction outperforming the Gaussian baseline at equal bit
budget by $4.8\times$ on average, qualitatively reproducing
\cite{dasgupta2017}. We stress that this is a reproduction in kind, not in
magnitude: the measured matrix has fixed dimensions, so the benchmarks of
\cite{dasgupta2017} cannot be passed through it, and our evaluation is at the
circuit's own scale on its own input modality.

\subsection{The Measured Wiring Confers No Advantage}

Against $\mathcal{N}_{\text{conf}}$ the measured matrix yields
$z = -1.59, -1.39, -1.47, -1.12$ for $k = 8, 16, 32, 64$, with $38, 35, 37, 36$
of $40$ null draws scoring higher. The point estimate is consistently
\emph{below} the null, though never significantly so.

Table~\ref{tab:sens} shows the protocol rejecting a degraded matrix
overwhelmingly. An \emph{improvement} of the size produced by
$\mathcal{P}_{\text{bal}}$ approaches but does not cross the threshold, which
is why we state a bound rather than an absence: any true advantage of the
measured wiring is below $1.8\%$ relative at $k=64$.

\subsection{Robustness}

The conclusion is unchanged under a fourfold change in graph density: at a
$\ge 5$ synapse threshold ($6.24 \times 10^6$ connections) we obtain
$z \in [-1.82, -1.12]$, against $[-1.59, -1.12]$ with no threshold. It also
holds for the contralateral hemisphere, for synapse-weighted rather than binary
$M$, and for Gaussian, low-rank and sparse synthetic inputs, with $z$ ranging
over $[-0.81, +1.25]$ and no consistent sign.

One asymmetry is worth recording. Measured fan-out is strongly non-uniform:
DA1, the pheromone glomerulus, drives 473 Kenyon cells while the quietest
drives 19. Both $\mathcal{N}_{\text{claw}}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{\text{bal}}$,
which flatten this, score \emph{above} the measured matrix at every $k$. To the
extent that the fly allocates disproportionate mushroom body real estate to
behaviourally privileged channels, that allocation appears to cost
general-purpose retrieval performance.

\section{Discussion}

Two readings must be kept apart. Our result is \emph{not} that
glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell wiring is random; Zheng \textit{et al.}
\cite{zheng2022} demonstrated otherwise, and our own fan-out asymmetry is
itself structure. The result is that the structure present confers no
measurable advantage on similarity search --- the computation the circuit has
been argued to implement.

This resolves rather than contradicts. The modelling assumption in
\cite{dasgupta2017} was not merely convenient but accurate \emph{for its
purpose}: with respect to retrieval quality, the measured matrix and a
degree-matched random one are interchangeable. Where a computation calls for a
random projection, evolution appears to have supplied one, and the deviations
from randomness that exist serve something else --- plausibly innate valence,
developmental constraint, or the over-allocation to privileged channels
visible in the fan-out.

More broadly, the availability of complete connectomes invites a class of
experiment that was previously impossible: taking a circuit-derived algorithm
and asking whether the measured substrate outperforms its idealisation. Our
protocol --- fix everything but the wiring, null out the degree sequences
separately, and calibrate what the test can see --- is not specific to
olfaction and transfers to any connectome-grounded computational claim.

\section{Limitations}

The claim is a bound, not an absence: advantages below $1.8\%$ relative are
not excluded, and tightening it requires more items, which costs
$O(n^2)$ in the retrieval evaluation. We test one task and one metric;
novelty detection \cite{dasgupta2018}, the circuit's other documented function,
is untested. DoOR covers 35 of 51 glomeruli, and the $4000$ queries are
mixtures of $172$ measured odorants rather than independent measurements.
MaleCNS is a single male animal; whether the conclusion holds across
individuals, or against the female FlyWire reconstruction
\cite{dorkenwald2024}, is open.

\section{Conclusion}

Substituting measured connectivity for the random matrix assumed in
\cite{dasgupta2017} leaves similarity-search performance unchanged to within
$1.8\%$, while the architectural advantage over classical LSH reproduces. The
mushroom body is a good hash function because of its shape, not its wiring.
Code, data pipeline and results are available at
\url{https://github.com/ssenge/FlyHash-Connectome}.
