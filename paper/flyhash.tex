\title{A Connectome Test of the Fly Hashing Algorithm}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sebastian Senge}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha (2017) described the \textit{Drosophila}
olfactory circuit as a locality-sensitive hash. Its glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell
projection was drawn at random because the wiring was unknown. We first rerun
their benchmark protocol (SIFT, GloVe, MNIST) with random matrices and recover
their result: on MNIST at hash length 4, LSH scores \ReplMnistLsh{} (reported
$0.160$) and the fly hash \ReplMnistFly{}, a $\ReplMnistRatio\times$ advantage
(reported $2.8\times$). We then run the same protocol through the measured
projection of the MaleCNS connectome, $\ConnGlom$ glomeruli and \ConnCells{}
Kenyon cells with every reconstructed connection. The advantage over LSH at
short hash lengths survives ($\ConnMnistRatio\times$ on MNIST), and the
measured wiring scores within \ConnRelMin{} to \ConnRelMax{} of uniform
rewirings with the same degrees. On mixtures of DoOR odorant profiles it scores
\PrimaryRel{} below them (two-sided $p=\PrimaryPtwo$; $90\%$ interval \BootLo{}
to \BootHi{}), a deficit that disappears when unmeasured responses are imputed.
The headline advantage depends on the budget: the fly network behind it uses
up to two orders of magnitude more operations than $k$-bit LSH, and LSH given
equal operations wins on all three benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
connectomics, locality-sensitive hashing, similarity search, replication
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) maps items to short codes under which similar
items collide with high probability \cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}. Dasgupta
\textit{et al.} \cite{dasgupta2017} noticed that the fly olfactory circuit
computes a related transform with three unusual features: a sparse binary
projection, an expansion rather than a reduction of dimension, and
winner-take-all. On SIFT, GloVe and MNIST it beat classical LSH by up to about
$3\times$. The projection was sampled at random, anchored to biology only by the
number of inputs per Kenyon cell, following the account that this wiring is
effectively random \cite{caron2013}.

That account has since been revised: in a whole-brain electron microscopy
volume, projection-neuron input to Kenyon cells is structured \cite{zheng2022}.
The MaleCNS connectome \cite{malecns2026} gives a complete measured projection
for a second animal. We ask whether the random matrix was a harmless
idealisation, and we do it in the order a replication requires:

\begin{itemize}
\item rerun the 2017 benchmark protocol with random matrices and check that it
reproduces the published numbers;
\item keep that protocol and data, substitute the measured projection, and
compare it with uniform rewirings that preserve both degree sequences;
\item repeat the comparison on odours, the input the circuit actually receives,
with a pre-specified inference protocol;
\item compare with LSH at explicit budgets: hash length, storage and operation
count.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Measured projection}

Let $M\in\{0,1\}^{d\times m}$ with $M_{ji}=1$ iff glomerulus $j$ connects to
Kenyon cell $i$. We build $M$ from MaleCNS v1.0 using every reconstructed
connection, with no synapse-count threshold. Projection neurons of class
\texttt{ALPN} are assigned to glomeruli by cell type; multiglomerular, unnamed,
thermo- and hygrosensory types are excluded. The right hemisphere gives
$d=\NGlomFull$ glomeruli and $m=\NCellsFull$ Kenyon cells with, on average,
$\InputsFull$ distinct glomerular inputs per cell. Because projection neurons
are aggregated by glomerulus this is not a claw count; cells have on average
$\PNPartners$ distinct projection-neuron partners.

\subsection{The 2017 protocol}

We follow \cite{dasgupta2017}: $10{,}000$ vectors per dataset (SIFT, $d=128$
\cite{jegou2011}; GloVe, $d=300$ \cite{pennington2014}; MNIST, $d=784$
\cite{lecun1998}); each input centred by its own mean; $1{,}000$ random
queries; for each, the true top $2\%$ ($200$) neighbours by Euclidean distance
and the predicted top $2\%$ by Euclidean distance between hashes; mean average
precision (mAP), averaged over \ReplTrials{} trials in which matrices and
queries change. The fly hash is $y=M^\top x$ followed by the $k$ largest
entries set to one. LSH is $k$ Gaussian projections kept as real values, the form
compared in \cite{dasgupta2017}; one-bit (sign) LSH scores much lower and is in
the supplement. The fly uses $m=20k$
Kenyon cells, which equates the operation count with LSH under the paper's
accounting ($d$ multiplications plus $d$ additions per Gaussian projection, one
addition per non-zero of $M$), or $m=10d$ as in its headline comparison. Two
details are not in the main text and are taken from the FlyLSH reference code
\cite{sharma2018}: each Kenyon cell samples $10\%$ of the inputs, and the tag is
binary. The paper computes mAP by its ref.~19; we use the standard definition
(mean precision at the ranks of true neighbours). The list-overlap score of the
reference code gives the same ordering and is in the supplement.

\subsection{The measured projection on the 2017 benchmarks}

$M$ accepts only $d=\ConnGlom$ inputs, so each dataset is reduced by PCA to
$\ConnGlom$ components. Features of images or words have no correspondence to
glomeruli, so each trial assigns components to glomeruli by a fresh random
permutation. Ground truth is computed on the reduced input, which is what every
hash sees. On that input we compare, over \ConnTrials{} trials, the measured
$M$; \ConnB{} null matrices (below); the 2017 construction at the same size,
in which every cell samples six glomeruli uniformly; and LSH. Each matrix's mAP
is averaged over trials and the measured matrix is ranked among the nulls.

\subsection{Null matrices}

The null is uniform over binary matrices with the same row and column sums as
$M$, sampled by curveball trades \cite{strona2014}, which converge to the
uniform distribution \cite{carstens2015}. It keeps how many glomeruli each cell
samples and how many cells each glomerulus reaches, and randomises only which
glomeruli share a cell. We use $30$ trade attempts per cell; glomerulus
co-occurrence $Q=\sum_{j<l}(MM^\top)_{jl}^2$ stays within three null SDs from
\PlateauSweeps{} sweep on, and retrieval scores at $30$ and $100$ sweeps agree
(supplement).

\subsection{Odour benchmark and inference}

Responses are the DoOR 2.0 consensus matrix \cite{munch2016} (commit
\texttt{\DoorCommit}), mapped from receptor to glomerulus. DoOR coverage is
uneven: $35$ glomeruli are measured for $51$ or more odorants, eleven for $12$
to $24$, and five for none. The baseline keeps the $35$ ($\ge40$ odorants) and
odorants measured on at least $12$ of them, leaving $\NOdorants$ odorants with
\MissingPct{} of entries unmeasured, set to zero; imputations and a
$46$-glomerulus variant are sensitivity analyses. The restricted projection has
$m=\NCells$ cells and mean $\Inputs$ inputs per cell. The benchmark is
$\NItems$ synthetic linear mixtures of two to five DoOR profiles; inputs are
normalised to unit mean as in \cite{dasgupta2017}; ground truth is the
$\kappa=10$ nearest items by Euclidean distance on raw responses; Hamming ties
are broken by a fixed random order, identical across matrices.

The primary hash size, fixed before this analysis, is $k=\PrimaryK$ ($5\%$ of
cells); $k\in\{8,16,32,64\}$ are secondary and Holm-adjusted. The rank of the
measured matrix among $B=\NullB$ nulls gives an exact randomization $p$-value.
The size of the effect across odorant sets is estimated by a two-stage
bootstrap that resamples source odorants, regenerates the benchmark and
re-draws $20$ null matrices per replicate ($\BootR$ replicates); its coverage
is checked by simulation. Equivalence is assessed at a margin of
$\pm\MarginPrimary$ relative mAP, fixed before this analysis.

The 2017 comparison sets the fly's $k$ active cells against $k$ LSH
projections. That matches operations only at $m=20k$. The headline network has
$m=10d$ cells sampling $d/10$ inputs each, about $d^2$ additions, against $2kd$

that many operations (\ReplOpsBitsMnist{} projections on MNIST) reaches mAP
\ReplOpsMin{}--\ReplOpsMax{} across the three datasets, above every fly score
(at most \ReplFlyMax{}). The same holds for the measured projection: LSH with
the operations of $M$ (\ConnOpsBits{} projections) reaches
\ConnOpsMin{}--\ConnOpsMax{}, above the measured fly at every $k$.

On odours ($d=\NGlom$) the fly hash beats sign codes at $k$ bits
($\ArchKbitsRatio\times$ at the primary size), trails at matched storage
($\ArchStorageRatio\times$), and at matched operations trails for small $k$ and
leads from $k=32$ ($\ArchComputeRatio\times$ at the primary size;
Table~\ref{tab:arch}). The advantage at $k$ bits is real and replicates; it is
an advantage per active cell, not per operation or per stored bit.

\section{Discussion}

The random matrix of \cite{dasgupta2017} was a harmless idealisation for this
task. Put in its place, the measured projection keeps the fly hash's advantage
over LSH at short hash lengths, and retrieves within two to three percent of
random rewirings with the same degrees, on image and word benchmarks as on
odours. Where it differs, it is slightly worse, in the direction reported for
odour discrimination in \cite{zheng2022}. The idealised construction does
somewhat better than either because its degrees are uniform; the measured
degree sequences, including the skewed fan-out, cost more than the pairing.

Two cautions apply. The odour deficit depends on treating unmeasured DoOR
entries as zero and is not detectable under any imputation. On the image and
word benchmarks the input has been compressed to $\ConnGlom$ dimensions and
assigned to glomeruli arbitrarily, so those results test the wiring's
statistics, not its odour-specific pairing. That the measured structure serves
other ends, such as innate valence \cite{zheng2022} or novelty detection
\cite{dasgupta2018}, is not tested here.

The larger qualification concerns the benchmark claim itself. Its headline
advantage is per active cell. Counted in operations, as \cite{dasgupta2017}
count them, the headline network is far more expensive than the LSH it is
compared with, and on those benchmarks equal operations reverse the result.
At the circuit's own input size the balance is closer: on odours the fly hash
leads sign codes of equal operation count from $k=32$.

\section{Limitations}

One animal, one hemisphere as the primary analysis, one task family. Our fly
scores on the 2017 benchmarks are below the published values, and some
protocol details were taken from reference code. The odour benchmark is
generated from partially observed profiles with linear mixing. The model is
binary and rate-free and ignores inhibition beyond winner-take-all.

\section{Conclusion}

The 2017 fly hashing result replicates with random matrices and survives the
substitution of measured wiring: the measured MaleCNS projection performs like
a degree-preserving random one, slightly worse at larger hash sizes. Its
advantage over LSH holds per active cell and not per operation. Code, data
checksums, results and a supplement are at
\url{https://github.com/ssenge/FlyHash-Connectome}.
