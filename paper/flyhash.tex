\title{A Connectome Test of the Fly Hashing Algorithm}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sebastian Senge}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha (2017) described the \textit{Drosophila}
olfactory circuit as a locality-sensitive hash whose glomerulus-to-Kenyon-cell
projection was drawn at random because the wiring was unknown. We test that
idealisation against four connectomes, MaleCNS, hemibrain, FlyWire and BANC:
four animals, seven hemispheres. We reimplement the 2017 benchmark protocol
(SIFT, GloVe, MNIST, plus odour mixtures) and recover its central pattern:
with random matrices the fly hash beats $k$ Gaussian projections at short hash
lengths ($\ReplMnistRatio\times$ in average precision on MNIST at $k=4$).
Substituting the connectome keeps that advantage, and retrieval stays within a
few percent of uniform rewirings that preserve both degree sequences, slightly
below them at large hash sizes. At equal connection count, spreading each
glomerulus's output evenly over Kenyon cells improves retrieval by up to
\CtrlOutMax{}; equalising inputs per cell does not. On DoOR odour mixtures the
MaleCNS connectome scores \PrimaryRel{} below its rewirings (two-sided
$p=\PrimaryPtwo$), a deficit that disappears when unmeasured responses are
imputed. Finally, the 2017 advantage is per active cell: real-valued Gaussian
projections given the same projection arithmetic as the fly network beat it on
every dataset.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
connectomics, locality-sensitive hashing, similarity search, replication
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) maps items to short codes under which similar
items collide with high probability \cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}. Dasgupta
\textit{et al.} \cite{dasgupta2017} noticed that the fly olfactory circuit
computes a related transform with three unusual features: a sparse binary
projection, an expansion rather than a reduction of dimension, and
winner-take-all. On SIFT, GloVe and MNIST it beat classical LSH by up to about
$3\times$. The projection was sampled at random, following the account that
this wiring is effectively random \cite{caron2013}.

That account has since been revised: projection-neuron input to Kenyon cells is
structured \cite{zheng2022}. Complete projections are now available from four
electron-microscopy connectomes \cite{malecns2026,scheffer2020,dorkenwald2024,bates2026}.
We ask whether the random matrix was a harmless idealisation for the task
that motivated it, in two ways:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Approach 1}: reimplement the 2017 protocol, check it against the
published pattern, add odour mixtures as a fourth dataset, and substitute each
connectome for the random matrix.
\item \emph{Approach 2}: an odour analysis adapted to the circuit, with
randomization tests, a bootstrap over odorant sets and an equivalence margin.
\end{itemize}

Both compare the connectome with the same null, with controls that change one
degree sequence at a time at equal connection count, and with LSH under three
budgets. To our knowledge no prior work has run the 2017 benchmark on a
connectome; \cite{zheng2022} studied odour discrimination, not retrieval.

\section{Common design}

\subsection{Connectomes}

Let $M\in\{0,1\}^{d\times m}$ with $M_{ji}=1$ iff glomerulus $j$ connects to
Kenyon cell $i$. For every connectome the same rule applies: uniglomerular
olfactory projection neurons are assigned to glomeruli by cell type
(multiglomerular, thermo- and hygrosensory types excluded), Kenyon cells are
those annotated as such on the same side, every reconstructed connection
counts (no synapse threshold), and cells without olfactory input are dropped.
Hemibrain glomerulus names are mapped to the current nomenclature using the
cross-dataset matches of \cite{schlegel2024}. We use MaleCNS v1.0 (male, both
hemispheres), hemibrain v1.2 (female, right only), FlyWire v783 and BANC v888
(female, both). All hemispheres share $\AnimCommon$ glomeruli; they have
\AnimCellsMin{} to \AnimCellsMax{} Kenyon cells with \AnimInMin{} to
\AnimInMax{} glomerular inputs per cell on average. The datasets differ in
imaging, synapse detection and proofreading, so part of any difference between
them is technical. The primary connectome is the MaleCNS right hemisphere
($d=\NGlomFull$, $m=\NCellsFull$; \PNPartnersFull{} distinct projection-neuron
partners per cell).

\subsection{Hash, null and controls}

The fly hash computes $y=M^\top x$ and sets the $k$ largest entries of $y$ to
one. Only $M$ changes between conditions. \emph{The null} is uniform over binary
matrices with the same row and column sums as $M$, sampled by curveball trades
\cite{strona2014,carstens2015}; it randomises which glomeruli share a cell and
keeps both degree sequences. We use $30$ trades per cell; co-occurrence
$Q=\sum_{j<l}(MM^\top)_{jl}^2$ stays within three null SDs from
\PlateauSweeps{} sweep on. Tests against it are approximate Monte Carlo
randomization tests.

\emph{Equal-connection controls} have exactly $\mathrm{nnz}(M)$ ones and change
one margin at a time: inputs per cell made as even as possible (fan-out kept),
fan-out per glomerulus made even (inputs kept), or both; the pairing is then
randomised by curveball. \emph{The 2017 construction} gives every cell six
uniform inputs; it changes both margins and the connection count.

\subsection{Scores, baselines and budgets}

Retrieval is scored by average precision at $n$, the number of true neighbours:
$\mathrm{AP@}n=\frac1n\sum_i \mathrm{prec@}i\cdot\mathrm{rel}(i)$, where true
neighbours never retrieved count as misses; recall@$n$ is reported with it.
LSH computes $k$ Gaussian projections kept as real values, as in the 2017
comparison, or their signs for bit codes. Budgets are \emph{per active cell}
($k$ projections for $k$ winners), \emph{per stored bit} and \emph{per
operation}. Operations are the projection arithmetic of \cite{dasgupta2017}:
$d$ multiplications and $d$ additions per Gaussian projection, one addition per
non-zero of $M$; winner selection, retrieval and any PCA fit are excluded.

The connectome keeps the fly hash's advantage over LSH at short hash lengths:
\ConnMnistReal{} against \ConnMnistLsh{} on MNIST at $k=4$
($\ConnMnistRatio\times$), $\ConnOdourRatio\times$ on odours
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bench}, Table~\ref{tab:conn}). LSH overtakes it from
$k\approx16$. Against its null the connectome lies within \ConnImgMin{} to
\ConnImgMax{} on SIFT, GloVe and MNIST; at the largest size it is below every
null draw on each dataset, and on odours it is below the null at every size.

The controls separate the two degree sequences (Table~\ref{tab:ctrl}). At
equal connection count, making fan-out even raises AP on image and word input
by \CtrlOutMin{} to \CtrlOutMax{}, increasingly with $k$. Making inputs per
cell even does not help (\CtrlInMin{} to \CtrlInMax{}). The six-input
construction behaves like the even-fan-out control. Removing row-centring,
which alters winner order only when inputs per cell differ, leaves the image
and word results unchanged but moves the odour results substantially: on
non-negative input the connectome's drive then tracks how many inputs a cell
has. What the circuit gives up relative to idealised wiring is thus
mainly its uneven use of glomeruli, not its pairing.

\section{Approach 2: odour analysis}
\label{sec:odours}

\subsection{Design}

Responses are the DoOR 2.0 consensus matrix \cite{munch2016} (commit
\texttt{\DoorCommit}). Coverage is uneven: $35$ glomeruli are measured for $51$
or more odorants, eleven for $12$ to $24$, and five for none. We keep the $35$
(a threshold of $40$ odorants, in that gap; our choice) and odorants measured on
at least $12$ of them: $\NOdorants$ odorants, \MissingPct{} of entries
unmeasured and set to zero. The benchmark is $\NItems$ synthetic linear
mixtures of two to five profiles, normalised to unit mean as in the circuit;
ground truth is the $\kappa=10$ nearest items on raw responses; AP is computed
over all $\kappa$ neighbours. The restricted connectome has mean $\Inputs$
inputs and \PNPartners{} projection-neuron partners per cell.

The primary size $k=\PrimaryK$ ($5\%$ of cells) and the family of secondary
sizes $\{8,16,32,64\}$ (Holm-adjusted) were fixed in an earlier revision of
this analysis, after first results had been seen; this is not a
preregistration. We compare $M$ with $B=\NullB$ nulls. A two-stage bootstrap
resamples source odorants, regenerates the benchmark and draws $20$ nulls
without replacement from the same pool of \NullB{} ($\BootR$ replicates); its
coverage was checked for one fitted rank-6 generator at $600$ items.
Equivalence is assessed at $\pm\MarginPrimary$.

representation and storage, so the two comparisons answer different questions.
The fly hash's advantage is per active cell; per operation it holds only
against one-bit codes in some settings.

\section{Discussion}

Across four animals the connectome behaves like a degree-preserving random
matrix for similarity search: it keeps the fly hash's advantage per active cell
and retrieves within a few percent of its rewirings, slightly worse where it
differs, in the direction reported for odour discrimination in
\cite{zheng2022}. Its pairing is structured but does not help this task. What
does matter at equal connection count is how evenly glomeruli are used: the
connectome's fan-out is skewed, and evening it out improves retrieval, whereas
evening out inputs per cell does not. On image and word input the random
matrix of 2017 is in that sense not only a harmless idealisation but a
somewhat better hash than the circuit; on odours the difference is small. That the skew serves other ends, such as innate valence
\cite{zheng2022} or novelty detection \cite{dasgupta2018}, is not tested
here.

Three cautions apply. The odour deficit depends on treating unmeasured DoOR
entries as zero and on the glomeruli included. Image and word input is
compressed to $\ConnGlom$ dimensions and assigned to glomeruli arbitrarily.
Differences between connectomes mix biology and reconstruction method.

The benchmark claim itself needs a qualifier. Its advantage is per active
cell. Counted as projection arithmetic, the headline network is far more
expensive than the LSH it is compared with, and real-valued projections given
equal arithmetic retrieve better on every dataset and dimension we tested.

\section{Limitations}

Four animals, one male. Our reimplementation departs from the published
absolute values; two protocol details come from later code, and the published
score is undefined. The 2017-protocol runs across connectomes use $50$ nulls
and are effect estimates; inference rests on the odour tests. The odour
benchmark is generated from partially observed profiles with linear mixing.
The model is binary and rate-free and ignores inhibition beyond winner-take-all.

\section{Conclusion}

The 2017 fly hashing result holds in a reimplementation and survives the
substitution of four connectomes, which perform like degree-preserving
random matrices, slightly worse at larger hash sizes. The circuit's
uneven use of glomeruli costs retrieval; its pairing neither helps nor hurts
much. The algorithm's advantage over LSH is per active cell, not per
operation. Code, data checksums, results and a supplement are at
\url{https://github.com/ssenge/FlyHash-Connectome}.
