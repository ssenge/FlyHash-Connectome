\title{A Connectome Test of the Fly Hashing Algorithm}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sebastian Senge}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha (2017) showed that the \textit{Drosophila}
olfactory circuit, modelled as a random sparse projection followed by
winner-take-all, is a locality-sensitive hash that beats classical LSH. The
projection was random because the wiring was unknown. We test it against four
electron-microscopy connectomes (MaleCNS, hemibrain, FlyWire, BANC; four
animals, seven hemispheres) and report three findings. First, the 2017 pattern
holds in a reimplementation of its protocol on SIFT, GloVe, MNIST and odour
mixtures: the fly hash beats $k$ Gaussian projections at short hash lengths
($\ReplMnistRatio\times$ in AP@200 on MNIST at $k=4$). Second, that advantage
is per active cell, not per operation: Gaussian projections given the same
projection arithmetic retrieve better on every dataset and at every input
dimension tested. Third, the connectomes behave like degree-preserving random
matrices, retrieving slightly worse than their rewirings (median
\AnimRelMedian{}; below the null on odours in all \AnimN{} hemispheres). The
cost lies in the skewed fan-out of glomeruli: at equal connection count,
evening out fan-out improves retrieval by up to \CtrlOutMax{}, while evening
out inputs per cell does not. The random idealisation of 2017 is therefore
justified, and slightly optimistic.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
connectomics, locality-sensitive hashing, similarity search, replication
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) maps items to short codes under which similar
items collide with high probability \cite{indyk1998,charikar2002}. Dasgupta
\textit{et al.} \cite{dasgupta2017} noticed that the fly olfactory circuit
computes a related transform (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}a): about $50$ glomeruli
project through a sparse binary matrix onto about $2{,}000$ Kenyon cells, and
inhibition keeps the most active $5\%$. On SIFT, GloVe and MNIST this fly hash
beat classical LSH by up to about $3\times$. The projection was random,
following the account that the wiring is effectively random \cite{caron2013}.

That account has since been revised: projection-neuron input to Kenyon cells is
structured \cite{zheng2022}, and four connectomes now give the complete
projection \cite{malecns2026,scheffer2020,dorkenwald2024,bates2026}. We ask
whether the random matrix was a harmless idealisation for the task that
motivated it; \cite{zheng2022} studied odour discrimination, not retrieval.
Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item a reimplementation of the 2017 protocol with documented provenance,
extended with odour mixtures, that recovers the published pattern;
\item a budget analysis showing that the fly hash's advantage is per active
cell and not per operation;
\item a comparison of the fly hash across four connectomes and seven
hemispheres, on the 2017 benchmarks and on measured odour responses, against
degree-preserving rewiring and against controls that change one degree
sequence at a time at equal connection count.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

The fly hash has been extended with dense pseudo-hashes and multi-probe
binning \cite{sharma2018}, and with projections learned by local plasticity
rules \cite{ryali2020,liang2021}; these change the algorithm, whereas we keep
it and change only the matrix. On the biological side, random convergence of
projection-neuron input was reported from anatomical tracing
\cite{caron2013}; electron microscopy later mapped the mushroom body
completely in the larva \cite{eichler2017} and in the adult hemibrain
\cite{li2020}, and found structured sampling in FAFB, where simulated odour
discrimination was worse than with random wiring \cite{zheng2022}. None of
these studies evaluated similarity search, the task behind the hashing
interpretation.


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Connectomes and matrices}

Let $M\in\{0,1\}^{d\times m}$ with $M_{ji}=1$ iff glomerulus $j$ connects to
Kenyon cell $i$. For every connectome the same rule applies: uniglomerular
olfactory projection neurons are assigned to glomeruli by cell type
(multiglomerular, thermo- and hygrosensory types excluded), Kenyon cells are
those annotated on the same side, every reconstructed connection counts (no
synapse threshold), and cells without olfactory input are dropped. Hemibrain
glomerulus names are mapped to current nomenclature via the cross-dataset
matches of \cite{schlegel2024}. We use MaleCNS v1.0 (male, both hemispheres),
hemibrain v1.2 (female, right), FlyWire v783 and BANC v888 (female, both). All
share $\AnimCommon$ glomeruli; they have \AnimCellsMin{} to \AnimCellsMax{}
Kenyon cells with \AnimInMin{} to \AnimInMax{} inputs per cell. The datasets
differ in imaging, synapse detection and proofreading, so part of any
difference between them is technical. The primary connectome is MaleCNS right
($d=\NGlomFull$, $m=\NCellsFull$).

Only $M$ changes between conditions. \emph{The null} is uniform over binary
matrices with the same row and column sums as $M$, sampled by curveball trades
\cite{strona2014,carstens2015} (30 per cell; co-occurrence
$Q=\sum_{j<l}(MM^\top)_{jl}^2$ is stable from \PlateauSweeps{} sweep on); it
keeps both degree sequences and randomises which glomeruli share a cell, and
tests against it are approximate Monte Carlo randomization tests.
\emph{Equal-connection controls} have exactly $\mathrm{nnz}(M)$ ones with
inputs per cell made even, fan-out made even, or both, and random pairing.
\emph{The 2017 construction} gives each cell six uniform inputs, changing both
degree sequences and the connection count.

\subsection{Hash, scores and budgets}

The fly hash computes $y=M^\top x$ and sets the $k$ largest entries of $y$ to
one. Retrieval is scored by $\mathrm{AP@}n=\frac1n\sum_i
\mathrm{prec@}i\cdot\mathrm{rel}(i)$ over the $n$ true neighbours, so neighbours
never retrieved count as misses, together with recall@$n$. LSH computes $k$
Gaussian projections kept as real values, as in the 2017 comparison, or their
signs for bit codes. Budgets are per active cell ($k$ projections for $k$
winners), per stored bit, and per operation, counted as projection arithmetic
as in \cite{dasgupta2017}: $d$ multiplications and $d$ additions per
projection, one addition per non-zero of $M$; winner selection, retrieval and
any PCA fit are excluded.

Table~\ref{tab:prov} gives the protocol and the source of each element; we add
$10{,}000$ mixtures of DoOR odour profiles as a fourth dataset. The 2017 paper
reports ``mean average precision'' without a formula. Precision averaged over
\emph{retrieved} true neighbours only, a convention in parts of the hashing
literature, reproduces its LSH baseline (MNIST, $k=4$: \ReplMnistLshRetr{}
against $0.160$) but not its fly score (\ReplMnistFlyRetr{} against $0.448$).
That score ignores misses, so we report AP@200 and recall@200 and compare with
the paper through orderings and ratios. With a connectome, $M$ accepts only its
own glomeruli: odours use the glomeruli DoOR covers, and SIFT, GloVe and MNIST
are reduced by PCA to $\ConnGlom$ components assigned to glomeruli by a fresh
random permutation in each trial, so these datasets test the wiring's
statistics, not its odour-specific pairing. Each connectome is scored against
$\ConnB$ nulls and one draw of each control per trial.

\subsection{Approach 2: odour analysis}

Responses are the DoOR 2.0 consensus matrix \cite{munch2016} (commit
\texttt{\DoorCommit}). Coverage is uneven: $35$ glomeruli are measured for $51$
or more odorants, eleven for $12$ to $24$, five for none. We keep the $35$ (our
threshold of $40$ odorants falls in that gap) and odorants measured on at least
$12$ of them: $\NOdorants$ odorants, \MissingPct{} of entries unmeasured and set
to zero. The benchmark is $\NItems$ linear mixtures of two to five profiles,
normalised to unit mean as in the circuit, with the $\kappa=10$ nearest items
on raw responses as ground truth. The primary size $k=\PrimaryK$ ($5\%$ of
cells) and secondary sizes $\{8,16,32,64\}$ were fixed in an earlier revision,
after first results had been seen; this is not a preregistration. MaleCNS is
tested against \NullB{} nulls with a two-stage bootstrap (resampled odorants,
regenerated mixtures, $20$ nulls drawn from the same pool per replicate,
$\BootR$ replicates) and a $\pm\MarginPrimary$ equivalence margin; every
hemisphere is tested against \AnimB{} nulls on its own and on the
$\AnimCommonOdour$ shared DoOR glomeruli.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The 2017 pattern holds}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_replication.pdf}}
\caption{The 2017 protocol with random matrices: AP@200 against hash length,
mean $\pm$ SD of \ReplTrials{} trials. Random tag selection performs like LSH;
winner-take-all at $m=20k$ and $10d$ beats both at short hash lengths.}
\label{fig:repl}
\end{figure*}

With random matrices the published ordering is recovered on SIFT, MNIST and
odours (Fig.~\ref{fig:repl}): random tag selection performs like LSH, as
\cite{dasgupta2017} argue it must, winner-take-all at $m=20k$ beats both, and
$m=10d$ does better still. The advantage is largest at short hash lengths
($\ReplMnistRatio\times$ on MNIST at $k=4$; recall $\ReplMnistRecallRatio\times$;
$\ReplOdourRatio\times$ on odours) and shrinks with $k$; on odours and GloVe
LSH overtakes the fly hash from $k\approx8$--$16$. Scoring against neighbours
on centred input, as the FlyLSH code does, leaves the MNIST ratio at
$\ReplMnistRatioCentred\times$. GloVe is close to chance at small $k$ for every
method.

\subsection{The advantage is per active cell, not per operation}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_budget.pdf}}
\caption{(a) MNIST reduced by PCA to $d$ dimensions, $k=4$: the fly hash's
lead over LSH per active cell grows with $d$, but LSH given the same projection
arithmetic as the $m=10d$ fly leads at every $d$. (b) Best AP of the fly hash,
with a random matrix ($m=10d$) and with the MaleCNS connectome, against
real-valued LSH given the same projection arithmetic.}
\label{fig:budget}
\end{figure*}

The 2017 comparison sets $k$ active cells against $k$ projections, which
equates projection arithmetic only at $m=20k$. The headline network has
$m=10d$ cells sampling about $d/10$ inputs each: roughly $d^2$ additions
against $2kd$ for LSH, a factor near $d/2k$ ($98\times$ on MNIST at $k=4$). A
sweep over input dimension on one dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}a) shows both
sides: per active cell, the fly hash trails LSH at $d=8$, ties at $16$ and leads
from $32$ on; given equal arithmetic, real-valued LSH leads at every $d$. The
same holds on all four datasets (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}b), for random matrices
(LSH \ReplOpsMin{}--\ReplOpsMax{} AP) and for the connectome, whose
$\mathrm{nnz}(M)$ additions buy $\ConnOpsBits$ projections that beat it on
\ConnOpsWins{} of \ConnNData{} datasets.


One-bit codes are weaker than real-valued projections, and against them the
comparison is closer (Table~\ref{tab:arch}): on odours the fly hash beats sign
codes of $k$ bits ($\ArchKbitsRatio\times$ at the primary size), trails at
matched storage ($\ArchStorageRatio\times$), and at matched operations trails
for small $k$ and leads from $k=32$ ($\ArchComputeRatio\times$).

\subsection{The cost lies in glomerular fan-out}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_controls.pdf}}
\caption{Degree controls, AP relative to the null mean. (a) MaleCNS right at
the largest hash size: the connectome, and matrices with the same number of
connections but inputs per cell even, fan-out even, or both; the 2017
construction changes the connection count. (b) The even-degree control and
the six-input construction in every hemisphere, mean over the 2017-protocol
runs.}
\label{fig:ctrl}
\end{figure*}

Why is random wiring slightly better? The controls change one degree sequence
at a time at equal connection count (Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl}a). Making fan-out even,
so that every glomerulus reaches the same number of Kenyon cells, raises AP on
image and word input by \CtrlOutMin{} to \CtrlOutMax{}, increasingly with $k$;
making inputs per cell even does not (\CtrlInMin{} to \CtrlInMax{}). The 2017
construction behaves like the even-fan-out control. In every hemisphere the
even-degree control beats the null, by \AnimBothMin{} to \AnimBothMax{} on
average (Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl}b). The measured fan-out is strongly skewed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}b; up to $\FanMax$ cells for \FanMaxGlom{} against a mean
near $195$), so a few glomeruli take part in many cells' drive. On odours the
same holds with smaller effects: even fan-out improves AP at every hash size
($+2\%$ to $+4\%$), while even inputs per cell helps only at the smallest
sizes. Row-centring, part of the 2017 protocol, alters winner order only when
inputs per cell differ; removing it leaves image and word results unchanged but
changes the odour results strongly at small $k$, where drive on non-negative
input then tracks the number of inputs (supplement).

\section{Discussion}

The random matrix of 2017 was a justified idealisation for similarity search.
Put in its place, four measured connectomes keep the fly hash's per-cell
advantage and retrieve within a few percent of degree-preserving random
matrices, slightly worse, in the direction reported for odour discrimination
by \cite{zheng2022}. Their pairing is mostly structured but does not help this
task; their fan-out is skewed and costs retrieval. That skew may serve other
ends, such as innate valence \cite{zheng2022} or novelty detection
\cite{dasgupta2018}, which we do not test.

The algorithmic claim needs a qualifier. The fly hash's advantage is per active
cell: real-valued Gaussian projections given the same projection arithmetic
retrieve better on every dataset and input dimension we tested. Against one-bit
codes, and at the circuit's own scale, the comparison is closer. The finding on
fan-out suggests an improvement, a fly hash with even glomerular fan-out at
equal cost, which we leave to future work.

\section{Limitations}

Four animals, one male; differences between connectomes mix biology and
reconstruction. Our reimplementation recovers the published pattern but not
its absolute values, and two protocol details come from later code. Image and
word input is compressed to $\ConnGlom$ dimensions and assigned to glomeruli
arbitrarily. The cross-connectome protocol runs use $\AnimBBench$ nulls and are
effect estimates. The odour deficit depends on treating unmeasured DoOR entries
as zero. The model is binary and rate-free and ignores inhibition beyond
winner-take-all.

\section{Conclusion}

The 2017 fly hashing result holds in a reimplementation and survives the
substitution of four connectomes, which behave like degree-preserving random
matrices and retrieve slightly worse because their glomerular fan-out is
skewed. The algorithm's advantage over LSH is per active cell, not per
operation. Code, data checksums, results and a supplement are at
\url{https://github.com/ssenge/FlyHash-Connectome}.
